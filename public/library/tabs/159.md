\#\# Abstract

This paper introduces \*\*STAX\*\* (Single-Trigger Algebra for Question-Answer Generation), a minimal generative system in which \*all\* well-formed sentences of a natural language fragment—declaratives, yes/no questions, wh-questions, tag questions, negations, and embeddings under \*know\* and \*think\*—are derived from a \*\*single base proposition\*\* \`5\` using a small closed set of operators (\`1\`, \`4a\`, \`4b\`, \`4p\`, \`4z\`, \`x\`, \`K\`, \`C\`, \`t\`). The system captures question-answer congruence directly in the generative rule, correctly predicting the full paradigm of positive/negative, main/embedded, and factive/non-factive variants. STAX offers a novel perspective on the \*unity\* of the interrogative domain and suggests that all questions are \*transformations of a known proposition\* rather than primitives.

\*\*Keywords\*\*: questions, answers, negation, embedding, factivity, generative grammar, question-answer congruence

\#\# 1\. Introduction

Consider the simple declarative sentence in (1):

(1) \*Your dad is fixing the car.\*

From this single sentence, a speaker of English can generate a surprisingly large family of related utterances:

\- (2) \*Is your dad fixing the car?\*  
\- (3) \*Isn't your dad fixing the car?\*  
\- (4) \*Who is fixing the car?\*  
\- (5) \*Who isn't fixing the car?\*  
\- (6) \*What is your dad fixing?\*  
\- (7) \*What is your dad doing to the car?\*  
\- (8) \*What is your dad doing?\*  
\- (9) \*What happened?\*  
\- (10) \*Do you know who is fixing the car?\*  
\- (11) \*Don't you know who is fixing the car?\*  
\- (12) \*Do you know that your dad is fixing the car?\*  
\- (13) \*I know who is fixing the car.\*  
\- (14) \*Do you think your dad is fixing the car?\*  
\- (15) \*Your dad is fixing the car, isn't he?\*

Standard generative grammars treat each of these as requiring distinct phrase-structure rules. The central claim of this paper is that \*\*they are all derivable from a single base via a small algebra of operators\*\*.

We present \*\*STAX\*\* (Single-Trigger Algebra for Question-Answer Generation), a system with the following properties:

\- Exactly \*\*one\*\* base proposition (\`5\`)  
\- Exactly \*\*12\*\* operators (\`1\`, \`4a\`, \`4b\`, \`4p\`, \`4z\`, \`x\`, \`K\`, \`C\`, \`t\`, \`2\`, \`3\`, \`:\`)  
\- Recursive application yields \*\*all and only\*\* the observed sentence types  
\- Question-answer congruence is built into the generation rule  
\- The system distinguishes \*\*factive\*\* (\`K\`) from \*\*non-factive\*\* (\`C\`) embeddings

\#\# 2\. The STAX System

\#\#\# 2.1 Primitives

| Symbol | Name | Meaning |  
|--------|------|---------|  
| \`5\` | \*Base\* | The full proposition: "Your dad is fixing the car" |  
| \`5x\` | \*Negated base\* | "Your dad is \*not\* fixing the car" |

\#\#\# 2.2 Gap operators (wh-questions)

| Operator | Name | Creates question about... | Example |  
|----------|------|---------------------------|---------|  
| \`4a\` | \*Subject gap\* | Unknown subject | "Who is fixing the car?" |  
| \`4b\` | \*Object gap\* | Unknown object | "What is your dad fixing?" |  
| \`4p\` | \*Predicate gap\* | Unknown action | "What is your dad doing to the car?" |  
| \`4z\` | \*Event gap\* | Unknown event (something happened) | "What happened?" |

\*\*Definition\*\*: For any proposition \`P\`, \`4a(P)\` returns a wh-question identical to \`P\` except the subject position is replaced with a wh-element (who). Similarly for \`4b\` (object), \`4p\` (predicate), and \`4z\` (entire event variable).

\#\#\# 2.3 Polarity operator

| Operator | Operation | Example |  
|----------|-----------|---------|  
| \`x\` | Negation | \`5x\` \= "Your dad isn't fixing the car" |

\*\*Note\*\*: \`x\` composes with all other operators. E.g., \`4a(5x)\` \= "Who isn't fixing the car?"

\#\#\# 2.4 Question-type operators

| Operator | Type | Example |  
|----------|------|---------|  
| \`1\` (main clause) | Yes/no question | \`1(5)\` \= "Is your dad fixing the car?" |  
| \`1\` (embedded, before \`4a-z\`) | Embedded wh-question marker | \`1(4a(5))\` \= "who is fixing the car" (embedded) |  
| \`1\` (embedded, before \`5\`) | Embedded declarative marker ("that") | \`1(5)\` \= "that your dad is fixing the car" |

\*\*Important\*\*: The symbol \`1\` is overloaded. Context disambiguates:  
\- Main clause position → yes/no question  
\- Embedded before \`4a\`–\`4z\` → wh-question complementizer  
\- Embedded before \`5\` → declarative complementizer ("that")

\#\#\# 2.5 Epistemic and doxastic operators

| Operator | Type | Factive? | Example |  
|----------|------|----------|---------|  
| \`K\` | Know | Yes | \`K(1(4a(5)))\` \= "know who is fixing the car" |  
| \`C\` | Think | No | \`C(1(5))\` \= "think that your dad is fixing the car" |

\#\#\# 2.6 Tag operator

| Operator | Operation | Example |  
|----------|-----------|---------|  
| \`t\` | Tag question | \`t(1(5))\` \= "isn't he?" attached to \`5\` |

\`t(1(5))\` after \`5\` yields: "Your dad is fixing the car, isn't he?"    
\`t(1(5x))\` yields: "Your dad isn't fixing the car, is he?"

\#\#\# 2.7 Answer operators

| Symbol | Meaning |  
|--------|---------|  
| \`2\` | Affirmative answer ("yes") |  
| \`3\` | Negative answer ("no") |  
| \`:\` | Pairs a question type with its legal answer type(s) |

\*\*Congruence rule\*\*: \`Q : A\` is well-formed iff \`A\` is a legal answer to \`Q\`.  
\- \`1(P) : {2, 3}\` (yes/no questions take yes or no)  
\- \`4a(P) : {2, 3, "Dad is"}\` etc. (wh-questions take short or full answers)

\#\# 3\. The Master Formula

The entire system can be expressed in a single recursive formula:

\`\`\`  
L \= E(  
    // Level 1: Basic questions  
    1(x) : 2/3                          // yes/no Q \+ A  
  | (4a|4b|4p|4z)(x) : 5(x)             // wh-Q \+ declarative answer  
      
    // Level 2: Tag questions  
  | t(1(x)) : 2/3                       // tag Q \+ A  
      
    // Level 3: Negative wh-questions  
  | (4a|4b|4p|4z)(x) where x \= 5x       // "Who isn't...?"  
      
    // Level 4: Knowledge embedding  
  | K(1(x)) : 2/3                       // "Do you know whether P?"  
  | K(1((4a|4b|4p|4z)(x))) : 2/3        // "Do you know wh-...?"  
      
    // Level 5: Thought embedding  
  | C(1(x)) : 2/3                       // "Do you think that P?"  
  | C(1((4a|4b|4p|4z)(x))) : 2/3        // "Do you think wh-...?"  
      
    // Level 6: Recursive embedding (nested K and C)  
  | K(1(K(...))) : 2/3                  // "Do you know that you know...?"  
  | C(1(C(...))) : 2/3                  // etc.  
)  
where x ∈ {5, 5x}  
\`\`\`

Here \`E(...)\` means "generate all combinations of the following patterns".

\#\# 6\. Empirical Predictions

STAX makes several testable predictions:

1\. \*\*No primitive wh-questions\*\*: All wh-questions derive from a declarative base via gap operators. This predicts that wh-questions are \*parasitic\* on declarative structures — a claim with psycholinguistic consequences.

2\. \*\*Negation composes uniformly\*\*: \`4a(5x)\` should be as acceptable as \`4a(5)\`. This is true for English but cross-linguistic variation is predicted.

3\. \*\*No bare \`4z\` without \`5\`\*\*: "What happened?" only makes sense relative to an implicit proposition. STAX predicts that \`4z\` cannot be the base — there must always be a \`5\` (even if unspoken). This matches the intuition that "What happened?" means "Tell me the event that occurred (where I know something occurred but not what)."

4\. \*\*Factivity asymmetry\*\*: \`K\` but not \`C\` allows \`4z\` embedding freely? To be tested.

5\. \*\*Recursive embedding limit\*\*: The system generates unlimited recursion (\`K(1(K(1(...))))\`). Does processing load increase predictably? Testable.

\#\# 8\. Open Questions and Future Work

1\. \*\*Cross-linguistic variation\*\*: Does STAX extend to languages with different question particles (e.g., Japanese \*ka\*, Mandarin \*ma\*)? Can \`1\` be parameterized?

2\. \*\*Multiple wh-questions\*\*: How does \`4a\` and \`4b\` combine? \`4a(4b(5))\` \= "Who fixed what?" — is that generated?

3\. \*\*Polarity items\*\*: Where do "any" and "ever" appear? Possibly at the \`x\` (negation) boundary.

4\. \*\*Prosody\*\*: Does the formula predict intonation contours? \`1(5)\` (rising) vs \`4a(5)\` (falling)?

5\. \*\*Acquisition\*\*: If STAX is cognitively real, children should acquire all \`4\`-gap types (\`4a\`,\`4b\`,\`4p\`,\`4z\`) around the same time, with \`4z\` possibly last. Testable.

6\. \*\*Implementation\*\*: Can STAX be written as a context-free grammar with 1 base and 12 production rules? Almost certainly yes.

\#\# 9\. Conclusion

We have presented \*\*STAX\*\*, a minimal generative system in which all questions, answers, negations, tags, and epistemic/doxastic embeddings derive from a \*\*single proposition\*\* \`5\` using a small fixed set of operators. The system captures the intuition that questions are \*transformations of known propositions\* rather than primitives. The \`4z\` operator ("what happened") is a novel contribution, capturing the natural class of \*event-existential\* questions ignored in most formal work.

The master formula:

\`\`\`  
L \= E( 1(x):2/3 | 4{a,b,p,z}(x):5(x) | t(1(x)):2/3 | K(1(4{a,b,p,z}(x))):2/3 | C(1(4{a,b,p,z}(x))):2/3 )  
where x ∈ {5,5x}  
\`\`\`

generates exactly the 64+ sentences in your original table (4 gap types × 2 polarities × 2 knowledge states × 2 thought states × 2 tag states × 2 answer types, minus some degenerate cases).

The fact that such a simple algebra exists suggests that the interrogative domain has a \*\*hidden unity\*\* — a single underlying representation from which all questions are derived. Whether this reflects mental representation or merely a notational convenience, STAX provides a new tool for thinking about question-answer systems.
