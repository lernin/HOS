\# A Framework for Contextual Relations: Perionyms

  

\*\*Prepared for Ashley\*\*

  

This report outlines a framework of contextual relations—\*\*Perionyms\*\*—which describe how concepts associate with one another from different observational perspectives. These relations map directly onto established areas of linguistic and cognitive research and are built upon a foundational distinction between two types of information: \*\*Phenonyms\*\* (observable elements) and \*\*Doxonyms\*\* (interpretive statements).

  

\---

  

\#\# Part 1: The Foundational Distinction — Lambanonyms

  

All contextual relations operate on \*\*registered content\*\*. Within this framework, the inputs to any relation are called \*\*Lambanonyms\*\*, which fall into two fundamental categories:

  

\#\#\# Phenonym — Observable Element

  

A \*\*Phenonym\*\* is an element that can be directly observed as part of a situation or reality context. Phenonyms describe the things, actions, and states present in the environment or conceptual scene. They answer the question: \*What is directly present?\*

  

\*\*Examples (related to "doctor"):\*\*

\- \`doctor → hospital\`

\- \`doctor → patient\`

\- \`doctor → scalpel\`

\- \`doctor → surgery\`

\- \`doctor → medicine\`

\- \`doctor → nurse\`

\- \`doctor → clinic\`

  

Phenonyms correspond to the observable structure of a situation. They are the elements that typically populate \*\*Peristonym\*\* sets.

  

\#\#\# Doxonym — Interpretive Statement

  

A \*\*Doxonym\*\* represents an interpretation, evaluation, or claim expressed in language. Doxonyms arise when language goes beyond describing what is present and begins to characterize, judge, or comment on the situation. They answer the question: \*What is inferred or judged?\*

  

\*\*Examples (related to "doctor"):\*\*

\- \`doctor → negligent\`

\- \`doctor → skillful\`

\- \`doctor → heroic\`

\- \`doctor → incompetent\`

\- \`doctor → famous\`

\- \`doctor → controversial\`

\- \`doctor → allegedly\`

  

These associations involve judgment or interpretation, not simply observation of the core elements of a situation.

  

\> \*\*Core Principle:\*\* \*Lambanonym defines what is taken in; Perionym defines how it connects.\*

  

\---

  

\#\# Part 2: The Four Core Perionyms (Contextual Relations)

  

With the distinction between Phenonyms and Doxonyms established, we can now define the four primary perspectives—\*\*Peristonym\*\*, \*\*Dromonym\*\*, \*\*Logonym\*\*, and \*\*Syndonym\*\*. Each organizes Lambanonyms within a different type of context.

  

\---

  

\#\#\# 1. Logonym — Language Context

  

A \*\*Logonym\*\* describes words that commonly appear together within language structure, such as in a phrase or sentence. To identify logonyms, one simply examines a sentence or phrase containing a word and observes what other words appear nearby. This corresponds to the linguistic concept of \*\*collocation\*\*, which studies words that statistically co-occur within a short window of text.

  

\*\*Key Characteristics:\*\*

\- Represents \*what is said\*

\- Includes both \*\*Phenonyms + Doxonyms\*\*

\- Typically the broadest of the Perionym relations

  

\*\*Examples (related to "doctor"):\*\*

\- \`doctor → patient\` (Phenonym)

\- \`doctor → examined\` (Phenonym)

\- \`doctor → treated\` (Phenonym)

\- \`doctor → said\` (Doxonym)

\- \`doctor → warned\` (Doxonym)

\- \`doctor → allegedly\` (Doxonym)

  

These associations arise because the words frequently co-occur in expression. Because sentences contain not only conceptual elements but also grammatical, narrative, and evaluative language, the logonym set is often the largest of the Perionym relations.

  

\---

  

\#\#\# 2. Peristonym — Situational Context

  

A \*\*Peristonym\*\* describes concepts that belong to the same real-world or conceptual context—a network of co-present elements within a situation. To identify Peristonyms, one imagines entering the situation associated with a concept and observing what elements are naturally present as part of that environment. This idea corresponds closely to research traditions such as \*\*Frame Semantics\*\* (Charles Fillmore) and \*\*Semantic Fields\*\* in lexical semantics, which model the conceptual situation surrounding a concept.

  

\*\*Key Characteristics:\*\*

\- Represents \*what co-exists\*

\- Composed primarily of \*\*Phenonyms\*\*

\- Forms a situational network of observable elements

  

\*\*Examples (related to "doctor"):\*\*

\- \`doctor → hospital\`

\- \`doctor → scalpel\`

\- \`doctor → nurse\`

\- \`doctor → patient\`

\- \`doctor → clinic\`

\- \`doctor → diagnosis\`

\- \`doctor → medicine\`

  

These associations arise from the environment or domain in which the concept exists, whether the context is concrete or abstract. The Peristonym set consists almost exclusively of phenonyms—elements that can be directly observed as part of the reality context.

  

\---

  

\#\#\# 3. Dromonym — Temporal Sequence

  

A \*\*Dromonym\*\* describes the typical course or sequence of events within a situation—an ordered progression of elements across time. To identify dromonyms, one considers the progression of events that normally occur. This idea corresponds closely to \*\*Scripts\*\* in cognitive science and artificial intelligence, introduced by Schank and Abelson (1977), to describe the expected sequence of events in familiar situations, such as visiting a restaurant.

  

\*\*Key Characteristics:\*\*

\- Represents \*what unfolds\*

\- Composed of \*\*Phenonyms in sequence\*\*

\- Forms a temporal progression rather than a static network

  

\*\*Example: The script for a doctor visit:\*\*

\`\`\`

appointment → waiting room → patient enters → examination → diagnosis → prescription → pharmacy → recovery

\`\`\`

  

Unlike Peristonyms, which form a network of related elements, dromonyms form a constrained, ordered sequence.

  

\---

  

\#\#\# 4. Syndonym — Expression Unit

  

A \*\*Syndonym\*\* is a fixed or conventional expression—a multi-word or patterned unit that functions as a stable linguistic form. To identify syndonyms, one looks for phrases that operate as single semantic units within language, resisting compositional interpretation.

  

\*\*Key Characteristics:\*\*

\- Represents conventionalized language

\- Functions as a stable, non-compositional unit

\- Often idiomatic or formulaic

  

\*\*Examples:\*\*

\- \`by and large\`

\- \`kick the bucket\`

\- \`spill the beans\`

\- \`in lieu of\`

\- \`how do you do\`

  

Syndonyms capture the formulaic dimension of language—the prefabricated chunks that speakers acquire and deploy as wholes.

  

\---

  

\#\# Part 3: Structural Relationships and Synthesis

  

\#\#\# Why Logonyms and Peristonyms Often Look Similar (and Why They Differ)

  

Because language frequently reflects real-world situations, the sets produced by Logonyms and Peristonyms often overlap. However, the two relations are not identical. The difference is explained by the Phenonym/Doxonym distinction:

  

| Relation | Composition | Example (doctor → ...) |

|----------|-------------|------------------------|

| \*\*Peristonym\*\* | Phenonyms only | \`hospital\`, \`patient\`, \`scalpel\` |

| \*\*Logonym\*\* | Phenonyms + Doxonyms | \`hospital\`, \`patient\`, \`allegedly\`, \`negligent\` |

  

The Doxonyms appear in language, but they are not directly observable elements of the real-world situation itself. This is why the Logonym set is often the largest.

  

\#\#\# Structural Relationship Between Perionyms

  

The four Perionyms relate to one another in terms of scope and constraint:

  

\`\`\`

Logonym ⊃ Peristonym ⊃ Dromonym

\`\`\`

  

\- \*\*Logonym\*\* = broadest (language: captures both observation and interpretation)

\- \*\*Peristonym\*\* = situational (observable network of co-existing elements)

\- \*\*Dromonym\*\* = most constrained (ordered sequence across time)

  

\*\*Syndonym\*\* operates orthogonally to this hierarchy, capturing fixed linguistic units that may appear within any of the other three contexts.

  

\#\#\# A Deeper Observation: The Foundations of Knowledge

  

What makes this framework powerful is that it mirrors three major cognitive structures identified in research, now unified under the Perionym umbrella:

  

| Cognitive Structure | Research Tradition | Perionym |

|---------------------|--------------------|---------|

| Situational Networks | Frames (Fillmore) | Peristonym |

| Event Sequences | Scripts (Schank & Abelson) | Dromonym |

| Language Patterns | Collocations | Logonym |

| Fixed Expressions | Formulaic Language | Syndonym |

  

In other words, the framework captures the primary ways humans organize knowledge: by association (network), by sequence (timeline), by language usage, and by conventionalized expression. The distinction between Phenonyms and Doxonyms clarifies how language expands beyond observation into interpretation, adding a layer of commentary and judgment onto our models of reality.

  

By "digging down to the basement," we've found that this simple framework—\*\*Lambanonyms\*\* (Phenonym + Doxonym) organized by \*\*Perionyms\*\* (Peristonym, Dromonym, Logonym, Syndonym)—aligns with some of the deepest structures studied in cognitive linguistics and AI knowledge representation.
