# **Toward a General Theory of Signaling**

*A Unified Framework Across Organisms and Artifacts*

---

## **Abstract**

Signaling is traditionally studied in fragmented domains—information theory, biology, linguistics, and engineering—without a unified framework. This paper proposes a general theory of signaling that integrates communication across organisms and artifacts. We introduce a multi-axis model consisting of **scope (autonym, genonym, xenonym)**, **signal type (intentional, instinctual, structural, autonomic)**, **carrier (embodied vs. artifact-mediated)**, and **storage (neural, genetic, external)**. By distinguishing **signals from cues** and separating **organismic systems from artifact components**, this framework enables consistent analysis of communication phenomena ranging from intracellular signaling to interspecies interactions and engineered systems. The model is grounded in established theories from information science, ethology, and semiotics.

---

## **1\. Introduction**

Communication is often narrowly defined in terms of human language. However, signaling pervades biological and physical systems at every level—from neuronal activity to animal behavior to engineered communication systems.

Existing frameworks:

* **Information theory** () defines signal transmission abstractly  
* **Cybernetics** () emphasizes feedback and control  
* **Ethology** () studies animal signaling  
* **Semiotics** () expands “sign” beyond language

Despite this, no unified taxonomy spans:

* internal biological signaling  
* inter-organism communication  
* artifact-mediated systems

This paper proposes such a framework.

---

## **2\. Core Axes of the Model**

### **2.1 Scope (Who is involved)**

* **Autonym** → signaling within a single organism  
* **Genonym** → signaling between members of the same species  
* **Xenonym** → signaling between different species

**Examples:**

* Autonym: neuronal signaling in the human nervous system  
* Genonym: spoken interaction between two humans  
* Xenonym: a bird interpreting the warning coloration of a toxic frog

---

### **2.2 Signal Type (How it operates)**

* **Intentional** → consciously produced signals  
* **Instinctual** → evolved behavioral patterns  
* **Structural** → always-on signals embedded in form  
* **Autonomic** → system-driven internal processes

**Examples:**

* Intentional: a person giving directions  
* Instinctual: a baby crying for care  
* Structural: warning coloration of poison dart frogs  
* Autonomic: hormone signaling or neural impulses

Ethological work (Tinbergen, Lorenz) demonstrates that instinctual signaling operates without conscious planning yet conveys functional information.

---

### **2.3 Carrier (What carries the signal)**

* **Embodied (Organism-based)**  
* **Mediated (Artifact-based)**

**Examples:**

* Embodied: vocalization, gesture, coloration  
* Mediated: written signs, rope signals between divers, digital devices

Artifacts extend signaling beyond biological limits, aligning with cybernetic systems theory (Wiener).

---

### **2.4 Storage (Where information persists)**

* **Neural** → stored in nervous systems  
* **Genetic** → encoded in DNA  
* **External (Artifact)** → books, symbols, recordings

**Important distinction:**

* Genetic storage encodes **biological constraints and patterns**, not symbolic meaning  
* External storage enables long-term persistence independent of organisms

---

## **3\. Signal vs. Cue**

A critical distinction:

* **Signal** → has a signaling function (may be intentional or evolved)  
* **Cue** → environmental information without signaling function

**Examples:**

* Rain → cue (no sender)  
* Frog coloration → signal (evolved to influence predators)

This aligns with biological signaling theory and avoids over-attributing “communication” to all environmental perception.

---

## **4\. Organisms vs. Artifacts**

### **4.1 Organisms**

* Integrated systems of signaling  
* Possess both emission and reception capabilities  
* Not usefully classified as “transceivers”

### **4.2 Artifacts**

* Can be specialized:  
  * **Emitter** (e.g., speaker)  
  * **Receiver** (e.g., microphone)  
* Or combined:  
  * **Transceiver** (e.g., radio device)

This distinction prevents category errors between biological and engineered systems.

---

## **5\. Case Studies**

### **5.1 Poison Dart Frog (Xenonym, Structural Signal)**

Bright coloration functions as a structural signal indicating toxicity. Predators respond instinctually, demonstrating evolved interspecies signaling.

### **5.2 Alligator–Bird Interaction (Cue vs Signal Boundary)**

An open mouth may function as a **behavioral cue** interpreted by birds as opportunity. Unlike the frog, the signal is not structurally encoded but behaviorally inferred.

### **5.3 Human Conversation (Genonym, Intentional Signal)**

Speech involves intentional signaling mediated through embodied carriers, with neural storage and cultural reinforcement.

### **5.4 Neuronal Signaling (Autonym, Autonomic Signal)**

Neurons transmit bioelectrical signals internally, forming the basis of all higher-level communication.

---

## **6\. Implications**

This framework enables:

* Cross-domain comparison of signaling systems  
* Integration of biology, engineering, and semiotics  
* Clear separation of:  
  * behavior vs. process  
  * signal vs. cue  
  * organism vs. artifact

It provides a foundation for:

* AI communication systems  
* bio-inspired signaling models  
* educational frameworks for teaching communication beyond language

---

## **7\. Conclusion**

Signaling is a universal phenomenon that transcends language and even conscious intent. By structuring signaling along the axes of **scope, signal type, carrier, and storage**, this framework offers a unified way to analyze communication across biological and artificial systems.

Rather than privileging human language, this model situates it as one instance within a broader and more fundamental system of information exchange.
