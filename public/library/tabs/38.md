White Paper 05

  

Morphology vs. Taxonomy

  

The Boundary Between Grammar and Concept

  

Author: Ashley

Date: March 20, 2026

Series: Foundations of the atomonym System

  

\---

  

1\. Abstract

  

Language operates at multiple levels. Words change form to express grammatical distinctions — plural nouns, past tense verbs, comparative adjectives. These morphological variations do not create new concepts. A cat is a cat whether one cat or many. Jumping is jumping whether in the present or the past.

  

Yet sometimes morphological derivatives do become new concepts. A "jumper" can be a sweater, not just a person who jumps. "Running" as a sport is different from "run" as an action. And a "jump" can be a physical structure (ski jump) rather than just the action of leaping.

  

This paper establishes the boundary between morphology (handled by grammar rules) and taxonomy (handled by atomonyms). It provides principled tests for distinguishing between inflectional variation and genuine conceptual novelty.

  

\---

  

2\. The Problem: What Belongs in the Tree?

  

Consider all the forms and meanings of "jump":

  

Form Type / Meaning In Taxonomic Tree?

jump base form (verb) ✅ YES

jump noun (a single leap) ✅ YES (different atomonym)

jumps 3rd person singular (verb) ❌ NO (grammar)

jumps plural noun (multiple leaps) ❌ NO (grammar — same noun concept)

jumps ski jumps / parkour jumps (structures/features) ✅ YES (if stable meaning, e.g., a constructed ramp)

jumped past tense ❌ NO (grammar)

jumping progressive aspect ❌ NO (grammar)

jumping noun (the activity/sport) ✅ YES (nominalized activity)

jumper agent noun (person who jumps) ❌ NO (compositional)

jumper sweater / garment ✅ YES (new concept, unrelated to jumping)

jumpy adjective (nervous) ✅ YES (metaphorical extension)

  

Intuitively, some belong, some don't. But what's the rule?

3\. Core Distinction: Inflection vs. Derivation

  

3.1 Inflection (Grammar's Job)

  

Inflectional morphology creates different forms of the same word to fit grammatical contexts. It does not create new concepts.

  

Category Example Base Relationship

Plural (noun) cats cat same concept, multiple instances

Plural (noun) jumps (multiple leaps) jump (noun) same concept, multiple instances

Past tense jumped jump same action, different time

3rd person singular jumps jump same action, different subject

Progressive jumping jump same action, ongoing aspect

Comparative faster fast same quality, different degree

Superlative fastest fast same quality, extreme degree

Possessive John's John same person, ownership relation

  

These are handled by grammar rules, not the taxonomy.

  

3.2 Derivation (Sometimes Taxonomy's Job)

  

Derivational morphology creates new words that may or may not represent new concepts.

  

Category Example Base New Concept?

Agent noun (-er) jumper (person) jump ❌ usually not

Agent noun (-er) jumper (sweater) jump ✅ yes

Noun (structure) jump (ski jump) jump ✅ yes (specialized meaning)

Adjective (-y) jumpy jump ✅ yes (metaphorical)

Abstract noun (-ness) happiness happy ð¤ borderline

Diminutive (-let) booklet book ð¤ maybe

Female (-ess) actress actor ❌ same concept, gender marked

4\. The Test: Compositional vs. Lexicalized

  

The same test from Paper 01 applies:

  

If the meaning can be understood by combining the base word with the suffix rule (or inflectional pattern), it does NOT get its own atomonym. Grammar handles it.

  

If the meaning requires its own dictionary entry (non-compositional, culturally stabilized, metaphorical, or a specialized concrete referent), it DOES get its own atomonym.

  

4.1 Applying the Test

  

Form Compositional? Meaning In Tree?

cats YES cat + plural ❌ NO

jumps (verb) YES jump + 3SG ❌ NO

jumps (plural noun) YES jump (noun) + plural ❌ NO

jumped YES jump + past ❌ NO

jumping (progressive) YES jump + progressive ❌ NO

jumping (activity noun) MAYBE "the sport of jumping" — conventionalized ✅ YES

jumper (person) YES jump + person who does ❌ NO

jumper (sweater) NO not about jumping ✅ YES

jump (ski jump structure) NO specialized physical object ✅ YES

jumpy (nervous) NO metaphorical extension ✅ YES

runner (person) YES run + person who does ❌ NO

runner (carpet) NO specialized meaning ✅ YES

runner (plant stem) NO specialized meaning ✅ YES

happiness MAYBE happy + state ð¤ (could go either way)

5\. The Grey Zone: Lexicalized Derivatives

  

Some derivatives hover in the boundary:

  

Form Status Reasoning

happiness Grey "The state of being happy" is compositional, but "happiness" as a philosophical concept may deserve its own node

kindness Grey Similar to happiness

booklet Grey "Small book" is compositional, but the word is conventionalized

duckling Grey "Young duck" is compositional, but culturally salient

piglet Grey Same as duckling

faster ❌ NO Clearly compositional (comparative)

fastest ❌ NO Clearly compositional (superlative)

  

Rule of thumb for grey zone: If the word appears in dictionaries with its own definition (not just a cross-reference to the base), it probably deserves an atomonym.

  

\---

  

6\. The Special Case: Nominalized Verbs (Gerunds)

  

Gerunds (-ing forms used as nouns) are particularly tricky:

  

Form Status Reasoning

running (activity) ✅ YES "Running is good exercise" — the sport/activity is a concept

running (operation) ✅ YES "The running of the engine" — different concept

painting (art object) ✅ YES "She bought a painting" — result noun

painting (activity) ✅ YES "Painting is relaxing" — activity concept

jumping (activity) ✅ YES "Jumping is her favorite warm-up" — activity concept

  

These do get atomonyms because:

  

· They function as nouns, not verbs

· They name activities or results that are culturally recognized

· They often have their own Hypernyms (sport, art form, product)

  

\---

  

7\. The Special Case: Concrete Specializations

  

Sometimes a word derives a concrete physical meaning from an action:

  

Form Status Reasoning

jump (ski jump) ✅ YES A constructed structure, not an action

jump (parkour jump) ✅ YES A specific obstacle/feature

run (ski run) ✅ YES A designated path/slope

slide (playground slide) ✅ YES A physical structure

swing (playground swing) ✅ YES A physical object

  

These get atomonyms because they refer to physical objects or places, not actions.

8\. Implementation in the atomonym System

  

8.1 Grammar Layer

  

Inflectional variations are handled by a grammar engine, not the taxonomy:

  

\`\`\`

Base atomonym: 7823 | run | verb\_move

Base atomonym: 7824 | run\_noun | instance\_of\_running

  

Grammar rules:

    - Plural (noun): run\_noun + PLURAL → "runs" (rule)

    - Past tense: run + PAST → "ran" (rule)

    - 3rd person: run + 3SG → "runs" (rule)

    - Progressive: run + PROG → "running" (rule)

\`\`\`

  

8.2 Derivational atomonyms

  

When a derivative qualifies for its own atomonym:

  

\`\`\`

Base: 7823 | run | verb\_move

  

Derived: 8945 | running\_noun | athletic\_activity

    derivation: from 7823 (run)

    derivation\_type: nominalization

    meaning: "the sport or activity of running"

    

Derived: 8946 | running\_noun | operation

    derivation: from 7823 (run)

    derivation\_type: nominalization

    meaning: "the operation of a machine or system"

    

Derived: 8947 | run\_noun | ski\_slope

    derivation: from 7824 (run\_noun)

    derivation\_type: specialization

    meaning: "a designated path for skiing"

\`\`\`

  

8.3 Linking Back

  

Derived atomonyms maintain a link to their source:

  

\`\`\`

8945.derived\_from: 7823 (run)

8945.derivation\_rule: -ing nominalization

  

8947.derived\_from: 7824 (run\_noun)

8947.derivation\_type: semantic\_specialization

\`\`\`

  

This allows:

  

· Etymology tracking

· Morphological awareness for learners

· Cross-linguistic alignment

· Understanding of how concrete meanings emerge from actions

9\. The Special Case: Zero Derivation (Conversion)

  

English frequently uses the same form for different parts of speech:

  

Word Part of Speech Concept In Tree?

run verb move quickly ✅ YES

run noun a jog ✅ YES (different atomonym)

run noun a series (run of luck) ✅ YES (different atomonym)

run noun in baseball (a run) ✅ YES (different atomonym)

run noun ski run ✅ YES (different atomonym)

jump verb leap ✅ YES

jump noun a leap ✅ YES (different atomonym)

jump noun ski jump (structure) ✅ YES (different atomonym)

  

These are different atomonyms sharing a string, not morphological variations. The test is: does the meaning change? If yes → new atomonym.

13\. Potential Objections and Responses

  

Objection Response

"This is too complicated" The alternative (putting every form in the tree) is far worse

"Where do you draw the line?" The compositional test provides a clear principle

"What about irregular forms?" Irregulars are still inflection (went = go + past) — grammar handles

"Doesn't this lose information?" The derivation link preserves etymology

"How do learners learn inflections?" Grammar layer teaches patterns; atomonyms teach concepts

"Why distinguish activity nouns from verbs?" They behave differently grammatically and conceptually

14\. Conclusion

  

The taxonomic tree contains concepts, not word forms.

  

· Inflectional morphology (plurals, tenses, comparatives) is handled by grammar rules

· Derivational morphology creates new atomonyms only when the meaning is non-compositional or culturally stabilized

· Zero derivation (same form, different part of speech) creates new atomonyms when the meaning differs

· Concrete specializations (ski jump, ski run) get their own atomonyms as physical objects/places

  

This keeps the taxonomy clean, the grammar layer systematic, and the system's behavior aligned with how humans actually understand language.
