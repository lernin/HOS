# Apatonym and Nomizonym

# **Apatonym and Nomizonym**

## **Misleading Names and Conventional Regard in a Reality-First Ontology**

### **Provisional Status**

This paper introduces two provisional Procedia concepts:

* **Apatonym**  
* **Nomizonym**

Both terms are currently marked with two stars in the Procedia Master list so that their names, definitions, and placement can be reviewed later.

---

## **Abstract**

Human language does not always describe things according to what they actually are.

Sometimes the **name itself** points toward the wrong kind of thing. A starfish is not a fish, and a guinea pig is not a pig. These are naming problems.

In other cases, the name is perfectly ordinary, but people conventionally regard or group the thing in a way that differs from its foundational Taxonym. A tomato is commonly treated as a vegetable in culinary contexts, even though its Taxonym places it among fruits. This is not a misleading name. Nothing about the word *tomato* suggests that it is a vegetable. Instead, the vegetable classification is attached to the tomato through convention.

Procedia introduces two separate concepts to represent these phenomena:

**Apatonym** — an established name whose wording misleadingly implies an incorrect identity or Taxonym.

**Nomizonym** — an identity, category, or grouping under which an Ontonym is conventionally regarded within a particular community, field, culture, region, or period.

These relations allow Procedia to preserve a reality-first taxonomic structure while also representing the ways language and convention interact with reality.

---

## **1\. The Reality-First Principle**

The foundational taxonomic tree should answer one central question:

**What is this thing?**

The answer should be determined by the nature and identity of the thing itself.

The tree should not be altered merely because people commonly use, group, sell, cook, describe, or understand the thing in another way.

For example:

Ontonym: TOMATO  
Taxonym: FRUIT

That is the foundational classification.

Other information may then be attached to the tomato node:

Common label: tomato  
Typical colour: red  
Common use: salads and sauces  
Nomizonym: vegetable  
Nomizonym scope: culinary practice

The tomato remains a fruit in the foundational Taxonym tree.

Its conventional treatment as a vegetable is stored separately as a relation attached to the tomato Ontonym.

This architecture prevents language, convention, regional practice, professional usage, and popular belief from distorting the underlying ontology.

---

## **2\. Why Two New Concepts Are Needed**

Two different phenomena are often confused.

The first occurs when a **name is misleading**.

The second occurs when a **thing is conventionally regarded as belonging to another category**.

These are not the same relationship.

Consider the expression *starfish*.

Name: starfish  
Actual Taxonym: echinoderm  
Taxonym implied by the name: fish

The misleading information is carried by the name itself.

Now consider a tomato.

Name: tomato  
Actual Taxonym: fruit  
Conventionally regarded as: vegetable  
Scope: culinary practice

The word *tomato* does not imply that the object is a vegetable. The alternative classification comes from convention, not from the wording of the name.

Procedia therefore requires two separate relations.

## **3\. Apatonym**

### **3.1 Definition**

**Apatonym:** an established name whose wording misleadingly implies an incorrect Ontonym or Taxonym.

An Apatonym directs the language user toward the wrong identity, parent category, or conceptual branch.

The problem is contained in the structure or historical meaning of the name.

The term combines a Greek root associated with deception or misleading appearance with `-onym`, meaning name.

---

### **3.2 Basic Structure**

An Apatonym relation normally contains four elements:

The established name  
The actual Ontonym  
The actual Taxonym  
The identity or Taxonym misleadingly implied by the name

For example:

Apatonym: starfish  
Actual Ontonym: sea star  
Actual Taxonym: echinoderm  
Misleading implication: fish

---

### **3.3 Examples**

#### **Starfish**

Apatonym: starfish  
Actual Taxonym: echinoderm  
Misleading implication: fish

The word *fish* is directly contained in the name, even though the organism is not a fish.

#### **Guinea pig**

Apatonym: guinea pig  
Actual Taxonym: rodent  
Misleading implication: pig

The established name implies a relationship to pigs that does not exist in the foundational taxonomy.

#### **Electric eel**

Apatonym: electric eel  
Actual Taxonym: knifefish  
Misleading implication: true eel

The animal resembles an eel in shape, but it does not belong to the true-eel branch.

#### **Koala bear**

Apatonym: koala bear  
Actual Taxonym: marsupial  
Misleading implication: bear

The word *bear* places the animal linguistically near bears, despite its different taxonomic identity.

#### **Black pine, when used for matai**

Apatonym: black pine  
Actual Taxonym: podocarp  
Misleading implication: true pine

The name linguistically suggests placement among true pines.

---

### **3.4 An Apatonym May Still Be a Valid Common Name**

An Apatonym is not necessarily an unusable or illegitimate expression.

A misleading name may be:

* historically established;  
* widely understood;  
* culturally important;  
* convenient in ordinary communication;  
* the most common label in a language.

For example, *starfish* may simultaneously be:

Ekpronym: starfish  
Apatonym: starfish → misleading implication of fish

The Ekpronym relation records that *starfish* is the common term.

The Apatonym relation records that the internal wording of the term implies the wrong Taxonym.

The label does not need to be deleted or replaced. Its misleading structure is simply made explicit.

## **4\. Nomizonym**

### **4.1 Definition**

**Nomizonym:** an identity, category, or grouping under which an Ontonym is conventionally regarded within a specified community, domain, culture, region, institution, or historical period.

Nomizonym is derived from the Greek root **nomiz-**, associated with regarding, considering, recognizing, or accepting something according to custom or convention.

A Nomizonym records how people conventionally treat or group a thing.

It does not determine what the thing actually is.

---

### **4.2 The Name Is Not the Source of the Classification**

A Nomizonym does not arise from misleading wording.

For example, the word *tomato* does not contain the word *vegetable*, nor does it linguistically imply vegetablehood.

The vegetable grouping is attached through culinary convention:

Ontonym: tomato  
Taxonym: fruit  
Nomizonym: vegetable  
Scope: culinary practice

The Nomizonym belongs to the Ontonym as a scoped conventional relation.

It does not belong to the name *tomato*.

---

### **4.3 Basic Structure**

A Nomizonym relation normally contains:

The Ontonym  
The conventional identity or category  
The domain or scope  
The community, institution, culture, or region  
An optional historical period

For example:

Ontonym: TOMATO  
Nomizonym: VEGETABLE  
Domain: culinary classification  
Community: general contemporary usage

---

### **4.4 Examples**

#### **Tomato**

Ontonym: tomato  
Taxonym: fruit  
Nomizonym: vegetable  
Scope: culinary practice

The tomato is treated alongside vegetables when preparing meals, organizing recipes, or arranging grocery departments.

#### **Peanut**

Ontonym: peanut  
Taxonym: legume  
Nomizonym: nut  
Scope: ordinary food classification

The peanut is commonly grouped with nuts because of its taste, texture, preparation, and use.

#### **Rhubarb**

Ontonym: rhubarb stalk  
Taxonym: plant stem  
Nomizonym: fruit  
Scope: culinary practice

Rhubarb is treated like a fruit in desserts, preserves, and pies.

#### **Coconut**

Ontonym: coconut  
Taxonym: drupe  
Nomizonym: nut  
Scope: ordinary language and food classification

Its common grouping as a nut can be represented without changing its foundational Taxonym.

#### **Whale in historical systems**

Ontonym: whale  
Taxonym: mammal  
Nomizonym: fish  
Scope: historical popular or legal classification  
Period: specified historical period

This records a historical convention without asserting that whales are actually fish.

---

## **5\. Scope Is Essential to a Nomizonym**

A Nomizonym should normally identify the convention under which the classification applies.

The statement:

Tomato → vegetable

is incomplete.

It could be misread as a Taxonym declaration.

A more precise representation is:

Tomato  
Nomizonym: vegetable  
Domain: culinary practice

Possible Nomizonym scopes include:

* culinary;  
* legal;  
* commercial;  
* medical;  
* educational;  
* religious;  
* cultural;  
* regional;  
* historical;  
* professional;  
* everyday popular usage.

The same Ontonym may possess several Nomizonyms under different scopes.

For example:

Ontonym: mushroom  
Taxonym: fungus  
Nomizonym: vegetable  
Scope: culinary practice

Procedia can therefore preserve useful human conventions without treating them as foundational reality.

---

## **6\. Apatonym and Nomizonym Are Different Relations**

An Apatonym belongs primarily to a **name**.

A Nomizonym belongs primarily to an **Ontonym within a convention**.

### **Apatonym example**

Name: starfish  
Misleading implication: fish  
Actual Taxonym: echinoderm

The error-like implication is carried by the word *starfish*.

### **Nomizonym example**

Ontonym: tomato  
Conventionally regarded as: vegetable  
Actual Taxonym: fruit

The word *tomato* is not misleading. The vegetable grouping comes from culinary convention.

The central diagnostic question is:

**Does the alternative identity come from the wording of the name, or from the way a group conventionally regards the thing?**

If it comes from the wording, it is an **Apatonym**.

If it comes from conventional regard, it is a **Nomizonym**.

## **7\. Relationship to Existing Procedia Concepts**

### **Ontonym**

The Ontonym represents the thing itself, independent of language.

Ontonym: the actual tomato

### **Taxonym**

The Taxonym records what the thing is within the foundational classification tree.

Tomato → fruit

### **Isonym**

The Isonym connects an Ontonym to a label in a language.

English: tomato  
Korean: 토마토  
Spanish: tomate

### **Ekpronym**

The Ekpronym records the common usage term in a particular language.

English Ekpronym: tomato

### **Apatonym**

The Apatonym records that the wording of a name implies an incorrect identity or Taxonym.

Starfish → misleading implication of fish

### **Nomizonym**

The Nomizonym records the category under which an Ontonym is conventionally regarded in a defined scope.

Tomato → vegetable  
Scope: culinary practice

### **Doxonym**

A Doxonym records an interpretation.

A useful distinction is:

Doxonym: an interpretation formed or registered by an observer  
Nomizonym: a socially or institutionally established way of regarding something

A personal or situational interpretation may be a Doxonym.

A stable conventional classification within a group or domain is a Nomizonym.

---

## **8\. Misconception Is Separate**

A misconception is an incorrect belief held by a person or group.

For example:

Belief: The Great Wall is visible from the Moon.  
Status: misconception

This belief does not necessarily involve a name or conventional classification.

The relevant concepts should therefore remain separate:

Apatonym: misleading name  
Nomizonym: conventional regard or grouping  
Doxonym: interpretation  
Misconception: incorrect belief  
Taxonym: foundational classification

A person may form a misconception because of an Apatonym, but the Apatonym and the misconception are not identical.

For example:

Apatonym: starfish  
Possible misconception: a starfish is a kind of fish

The Apatonym exists in the language.

The misconception exists in the mind of the observer.

## **10\. Benefits for Procedia**

### **Protection of the Taxonomic Tree**

The foundational tree remains based only on what things are.

### **Clear Treatment of Misleading Names**

Terms such as *starfish* and *guinea pig* can remain common labels while their misleading implications are explicitly represented.

### **Representation of Human Convention**

Culinary, legal, commercial, historical, regional, and cultural groupings can be preserved without being mistaken for foundational taxonomy.

### **Better Educational Explanations**

A learner can be shown:

What the thing is  
What it is commonly called  
What the name misleadingly implies  
How people conventionally regard it

### **Improved Artificial-Intelligence Reasoning**

An AI system using Procedia can distinguish among:

Actual identity  
Foundational Taxonym  
Common label  
Misleading name  
Conventional grouping  
Observer interpretation  
Incorrect belief

This reduces category confusion and produces more precise explanations.

---

## **11\. Conclusion**

Apatonym and Nomizonym represent two fundamentally different relationships between language and reality.

An **Apatonym** is a name whose wording misleadingly points toward the wrong identity or Taxonym.

A **Nomizonym** is a category or identity under which an Ontonym is conventionally regarded within a defined social, cultural, professional, regional, or historical context.

A starfish has an Apatonym because its name misleadingly identifies it as a fish.

A tomato has a Nomizonym because it is conventionally regarded as a vegetable in culinary contexts, even though the word *tomato* itself is not misleading.

Neither relation changes what the thing is.

Both are attached to the reality-based Ontonym as explicit language or convention relations.

This preserves the central Procedia principle:

**Reality determines the foundational tree. Names and conventions are attached to that reality without replacing it.**
