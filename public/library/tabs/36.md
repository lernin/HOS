White Paper 03

  

Idiom Atomicity

  

Non-Compositional Meaning as First-Class Citizens

  

Author: Ashley

Date: March 20, 2026

Series: Foundations of the atomonym System

  

\---

  

1\. Abstract

  

Natural language is filled with expressions whose meaning cannot be derived from their component parts. "Kick the bucket" has nothing to do with kicking or buckets. "Spill the beans" does not involve legumes. "Hold your tongue" does not describe a physical grip on a body part.

  

Traditional NLP systems attempt to parse these compositionally, leading to hallucinations, translation errors, and confused learners. This paper proposes that idioms be treated as atomic units — single atomonyms with their own meaning, Hypernyms, and grammatical properties — entirely bypassing the attempt to decompose them.

  

\---

  

2\. The Problem with Compositional Parsing

  

2.1 The Standard Approach

  

Most language systems see:

  

"He kicked the bucket."

  

And parse:

  

\`\`\`

\[He\] \[kicked\] \[the\] \[bucket\]

   ↑      ↑       ↑       ↑

pronoun  verb  article   noun

\`\`\`

  

Then they try to combine the meanings:

kick + the + bucket → ??

  

The result is nonsense, or worse — a confident wrong answer.

  

2.2 The Failure Modes

  

System Behavior Result

Literal interpretation "He struck a cylindrical container with his foot" ❌

Statistical guess "He died" (sometimes correct, sometimes not)

Hallucination "He was angry" or other invented meaning ❌

Translation "그는 양동이를 찼다" (literally "he kicked a bucket") ❌

  

None of these are acceptable in a system that aims for precision.

  

\---

  

3\. Core Insight: Idioms Are Atoms

  

An idiom is not a phrase to be parsed. It is a single semantic unit that happens to be spelled with multiple words.

  

Just as "bat" (the animal) and "bat" (the equipment) are different atomonyms sharing a string, "kick the bucket" is one atomonym whose string happens to contain spaces.

  

\`\`\`

4522 | bat | sports\_equipment

4521 | bat | mammal

  

8901 | kick\_the\_bucket | die

\`\`\`

  

The system never attempts to break 8901 into components. It simply knows: this string maps to this concept.

4\. Types of Idiomatic Expressions

  

4.1 Pure Idioms (Non-Compositional)

  

Idiom atomonym Hypernym

let the cat out of the bag 8904 reveal\_secret

hit the sack 8905 go\_to\_sleep

These have no literal relationship to their component words.

  

4.2 Phrasal Verbs (Often Idiomatic)

  

Phrasal Verb atomonym Hypernym

give up 8911 quit

turn down 8912 reject

look up 8913 search

run into 8914 encounter

put up with 8915 tolerate

  

These are compositional in origin but have conventionalized meanings that may not be transparent.

  

4.3 Fixed Expressions

  

Expression atomonym Hypernym

by and large 8921 generally

once in a blue moon 8922 rarely

in the meantime 8923 meanwhile

all of a sudden 8924 suddenly

  

These function as single adverbial units.

  

4.4 Proverbs (Discourse-Level Units)

  

Proverb atomonym Hypernym

don't count your chickens before they hatch 8932 avoid\_premature\_optimism

a rolling stone gathers no moss 8933 movement\_prevents\_settling

  

These could be stored as units, though they may also be parsed for analysis.

5\. The Plektonym Distinction (Preview)

  

As discussed in our conversation, some idioms encode multiple concepts in one unit:

  

Idiom Components Type

kick the bucket \[die\] Haplonym (single concept)

hold your tongue \[be\_quiet\] + \[stop\_speaking\] + \[implied\_reproach\] Plektonym (composite)

  

This will be explored fully in Paper 04: Plektonyms. For now, we note that both types are atomic in the sense that they are not parsed compositionally.

6\. Implementation in the atomonym System

  

6.1 atomonym Structure for Idioms

  

\`\`\`

8901 | kick\_the\_bucket | die

    register: informal, euphemistic, humorous

    literal\_meaning: null

    grammar: verb\_phrase

    components: null  (for haplonym idioms)

    

8956 | hold\_your\_tongue | directive\_speech\_act

    register: idiomatic, directive

    literal\_meaning: null

    grammar: verb\_phrase

    components: \[

        { concept: be\_quiet, role: primary },

        { concept: stop\_speaking, role: secondary },

        { concept: implied\_reproach, role: pragmatic }

    \]

\`\`\`

  

6.2 Parsing Behavior

  

When the system encounters text:

  

"My grandfather kicked the bucket last year."

  

It tokenizes, but the tokenizer is idiom-aware:

  

\`\`\`

\[My\] \[grandfather\] \[kick\_the\_bucket\] \[last\] \[year\]

\`\`\`

  

Not:

  

\`\`\`

\[My\] \[grandfather\] \[kicked\] \[the\] \[bucket\] \[last\] \[year\]

\`\`\`

  

The idiom is recognized as a single token.

  

6.3 Idiom Recognition

  

Recognition can happen via:

  

1\. Lookup table — known idioms mapped to atomonyms

2\. Contextual detection — when literal parsing fails, consider idiom

3\. Statistical signals — unusual collocations flagged as potential idioms

4\. Human curation — for high-importance idioms

7\. The Idiom Preference Ratio (IPR)

  

From our earlier discussion of the Adaptive Communicator, the Idiom Preference Ratio measures how often an idiom is used vs. its literal alternative:

  

\`\`\`

IPR = idiom\_usage / (idiom\_usage + base\_expression\_usage)

\`\`\`

  

For "kick the bucket":

  

· idiom\_usage = occurrences of "kick the bucket"

· base\_expression\_usage = occurrences of "die" (in similar contexts)

  

IPR varies by:

  

· Language (English higher than Korean for many idioms)

· Register (informal contexts higher)

· Individual style

  

This becomes important for translation decisions: preserve the idiom if target language IPR is within tolerance.

  

\---

  

8\. Why This Matters

  

System Component Impact

Parsing accuracy No more literal misinterpretations

Translation quality Idioms map to appropriate target equivalents, not word-for-word

Hallucination reduction System never invents meanings from component words

Learning tracking Student use of idioms is tracked as concept mastery, not word knowledge

Register awareness System knows which idioms are formal/informal

Cross-language alignment Idiom atomonyms can link to equivalent idioms in other languages

11\. Potential Objections and Responses

  

Objection Response

"This blows up the atomonym count" Idioms are finite (thousands, not millions). Worth the accuracy gain.

"How do you handle novel idioms?" New idioms can be added as encountered, with human review.

"What about semi-compositional phrases?" Borderline cases can be handled by confidence scores and human curation.

"Doesn't this lose information?" The literal meaning is often irrelevant. For plektonyms, components are preserved internally.

"How does translation work?" Idiom atomonyms link to equivalent idioms in target languages via concept mapping.

  

\---

  

12\. Conclusion

  

Idioms are not phrases to be parsed. They are atomic meaning units that happen to be written with spaces.

  

By treating them as first-class atomonyms, the atomonym system:

  

· Eliminates a major source of hallucinations

· Enables accurate translation

· Supports proper learner tracking

· Preserves register and pragmatic information

  

The idiom is not the exception. It is another kind of atom.
