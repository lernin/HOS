# Ocean Estate Design Vocabulary

Status: provisional, tested through the Design Lab before promotion into production.

## Core idea
Ocean Estate should feel like one designed world, not a collection of unrelated assets. Every structural, natural, furnishing, lighting, and landscape decision should inherit a small shared visual grammar.

## 1. Structures
- Warm mineral massing: travertine, cream limestone, pale plaster, restrained marble.
- Dark bronze is the preferred metal accent; use it as a reveal, edge, frame, cap, or shadow line rather than broad cladding.
- Natural walnut/oak provides warmth where touch, shelter, or domestic scale is useful.
- Geometry is disciplined and calm, but edges can be softened or layered to avoid programmer-box flatness.
- Columns, walls, furniture, and built objects must visibly land on a floor, plinth, footing, or base. No accidental floating.
- Integrated light is part of the architecture: recessed blades, concealed strips, wall washing, stair-edge light. Avoid a field of visible generic fixtures.
- One hero gesture per area; supporting forms stay quiet.

## 2. Natural objects
- Nature should be recognizably organic but art-directed, never random noise.
- Prefer asymmetry, varied silhouette, visible branch logic, and layered foliage.
- Keep enough negative space to read trunks and branches.
- Use a restrained green family: deep green, botanical green, and occasional lighter/sage accents.
- Repetition should vary scale, rotation, bend, and density while preserving a family resemblance.
- Plants must meet soil, beds, planters, or paving intentionally; no unexplained clipping.
- Specimen trees may become architectural focal points, but ordinary planting should support rather than compete with the building.

## 3. Settings
- Interior/exterior transitions should feel continuous: stone, timber, bronze, glass, water, and planting repeat across boundaries.
- Paving and landscape beds need clear edges and believable thickness/depth.
- Water edges should be deliberate architectural lines, not exposed geometry intersections.
- Furniture clusters should feel composed and usable, with circulation space around them.
- Lighting hierarchy: ambient environment first, architectural light second, decorative light last.
- Scene density should be layered rather than cluttered: foreground detail, midground structure, background landscape.

## 4. Cross-cutting rules
- Soft geometry, hard discipline.
- Warm mineral + dark bronze + natural timber + deep green.
- Ground every object.
- Asymmetry in nature; restraint in architecture.
- Luxury comes from proportion, material, light, and detail—not ornament overload.
- One hero gesture per area.
- If an object does not clearly belong to this vocabulary, test it in the Design Lab before using it in the estate.

## 5. Design Lab workflow
Each gallery is experimental until Ashley selects a preferred direction.

- Canonical: approved design language or asset family.
- Experimental: candidate currently under review.
- Rejected: intentionally not reused.

The Tree Styles gallery is the first explicit vocabulary test: one underlying tree expressed through nine different design languages so that we can select how an "Ocean Estate tree" should look before propagating that language across the landscape.
